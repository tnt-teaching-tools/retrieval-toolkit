START WITH THE PRINCIPLE

Retrieving is part of learning.

Retrieval practice means deliberately bringing previously learned knowledge to mind. An independent attempt followed by a check can reveal gaps and support later remembering. The Learning Scientists’ introduction ↗

Everyone retrieves

Build in an individual attempt before sharing or seeing an answer.

Feedback matters

Verify accuracy, explain mistakes and give students time to improve.

Return to older learning

Revisit knowledge across lessons and weeks, with appropriate support.

Keep the stakes low

Make this a normal learning routine. Adjust challenge so students can succeed.

WHAT THE RESEARCH SAYS

Evidence behind the practice.

Teacher-friendly summaries
ROEDIGER & KARPICKE · 2006

Recall can improve later retention

In experiments using prose passages, retrieval produced stronger delayed recall than repeated study, even though study could look better immediately.

Try: judge learning after a delay, not only at the end of today’s task.

Read the retrieval practice explainer ↗
DUNLOSKY ET AL. · 2013

Practice testing and spacing

This review rated practice testing and distributed practice as broadly useful learning techniques across the evidence it examined.

Try: spread retrieval across time, rather than saving it for revision week.

Read the summary for teachers ↗
AGARWAL, NUNES & BLUNT · 2021

Benefits in real classrooms

A review of 50 classroom experiments found benefits across different subjects and education levels. Most experiments were in Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic settings.

Try: check how well your implementation supports your particular learners.

Read the authors’ classroom summary ↗
KARPICKE & BLUNT · 2011

Beyond isolated facts

In their study, retrieval supported meaningful learning, including inference questions, and outperformed the concept-mapping study condition.

Try: retrieve explanations and relationships as well as definitions.

Read the concept-mapping explainer ↗
The activity is a vehicle. Evidence for retrieval practice does not establish that every wheel, game or worksheet is equally effective. Choose the format that gets everyone recalling useful knowledge, checking accuracy and revisiting gaps.

A SIMPLE CLASSROOM ROUTINE

Choose · Recall · Check · Revisit

  1. Choose taught knowledge. Include prerequisites and older learning, not only the previous lesson.
  2. Let everyone attempt. Close notes at first. Allow enough thinking time and offer cues when needed.
  3. Check and improve. Use trusted answers; students correct in another colour or record a clearer explanation.
  4. Plan the next revisit. Use the gaps you noticed to decide what to return to.
